## Why I tried this

At Evonik I spent more than three years around NIR: feedstuff analysis, prediction algorithms, two patents. Years later, at Eurofins, I built automated ML pipelines that take the hand work out of ADMET models. Putting the two together was the obvious next step. Can the AutoML approach build NIR calibrations?

The classic way is still a person at a desk: pretreat the spectra, look at a PCA, fit a PLS model, tune it, repeat. It works, and it takes an expert and a lot of time per property. I wanted to see how close a pipeline gets with one upload and a one-hour time limit.

## I expected better

I thought the pipeline would win more often. On the 6 properties with a published split, the published error is lower 5 times, by 1% to 17%. So I downloaded the papers behind those numbers and read how each one was made.

## What the papers show

- **NIRsoil, 3 properties (published lower by 1–3%).** The numbers come from a 2006 contest (Fernández Pierna & Dardenne 2008). Three participants predicted the test set blind. The best published errors are the organisers' own models, and the organisers held the test reference values. Against the best blind entry, the pipeline is lower on all three: 0.576 against 0.654 for nitrogen, 0.439 against 0.558 for carbon, 3.38 against 3.58 for CEC.
- **Gasoline octane (published lower by 16%).** The split and the 0.234 come from the tutorial of the R package pls, not from the dataset paper; Kalivas 1997 used a different split. The tutorial prints the test error for 1 to 10 PLS components, and I took the lowest, 0.234. That flatters the published side: the tutorial itself picks 2 components by cross-validation, which gives 0.244. The test set has 10 samples, so one sample moves the error a lot.
- **Tecator fat (published lower by 17%).** The 0.36 comes from a Bayesian neural network that Thodberg, who also published the dataset, built for this data. The pipeline's 0.435 beats the models in the original paper (Borggaard & Thodberg 1992: neural network 0.65, PLS 2.86). I have not read the 1996 paper behind 0.36 yet, so I can't say how that model was chosen.
- **Tecator protein (pipeline lower by 24%).** The 0.52 is a side result in a statistics paper (Bergsma 2017), from a single local optimum. Zhao et al. 2012 note that the protein reference values are known to be imprecise.

So most of the gap is how the published numbers were obtained. The one clear loss to a better method is Tecator fat.

## What the pipeline does have

It needs no chemometrics expert to run, it gives every property a validated model with a report in an hour, and it deploys as an API. For a lab with many properties and little time, that matters as much as the last few percent.

## Can the published numbers be reproduced?

Only 6 of the 15 published results come with a published train/test split. For the other 9 I cannot rerun what the authors did, so those rows stay indicative. And as the papers show, even the 6 were not all made blind. A published error you cannot reproduce is hard to beat and hard to trust.

That is why I want more data. Spectra with reference values, ideally with the split that was used, make each comparison fairer. If you have some, the box at the end says how to send it.
